# Make the ritual the hero, not the gum

Kids' brands that last talk to children like a cheeky friend and to parents like a trustworthy adult. They hang that double voice on one **repeatable ritual the audience owns**: the Carambar joke, the Malabar tattoo and biggest-bubble contest, the Bazooka Joe comic, Oreo's twist-lick-dunk. A mascot comes and goes; the ritual and the sound stay. For a candy brand, the rules now push the same way. ARPP's v4 eating-behaviour code (in force since 15 June 2026) explicitly covers imaginary characters. The EU Pledge counts YouTube and brand-owned social channels as advertising media. The UK has banned paid online ads for identifiable "less healthy" products since 5 January 2026. YouTube demotes kids' content that heavily promotes products. Taken together, the safest and most effective version of "Les Aventures Boum Boum" is **brand entertainment whose story and ritual never need the product to work**. The gum stays an occasional, shared, honest presence, never the thing that solves the hero's problem. Your standing rule (one idea per episode, a real choice, a meaningful ending) matches what the evidence says makes kids' series deep, and it is also what keeps a candy series compliant. The weak spots are the "Fais BOUM !" end card (an imperative addressed to children) and flavour-specific episodes like Ep. 7. Both are fixable. The evidence base is strong on rules and on French candy tone, and thin on measured effectiveness, on Big Babol and other competitors in France and MENA, and on the final status of Saudi and Maghreb rules.

**How to read this report.** Claims with a linked source are evidence drawn from the research notes. Sentences beginning "Inference:" or framed as recommendations are my reasoning from that evidence. Several sources are company or agency self-reports, and I flag them. This is not legal advice; Section 4 ends with a recommendation to get pre-clearance.

## French candy brands speak "tu", puns and playground lore, while global brands speak to the inner child

The French bubble-gum and candy voice is a conspiratorial kid-to-kid "copain" register. Malabar's own brand page opens with "Évidemment, tu connais Malabar !" and promises "la dose de bonne humeur assurée pour des journées qui déchirent !". Its signature line is "Le Bubble gum qui donne le sourire" / "Des bulles de sourire". The same page writes playground rituals into the brand story: "Qui n'a jamais surenchéri au concours de la plus grosse bulle ? Dans les cours de récré, c'est même devenu une tradition" ([Carambar & Co – Malabar](https://carambarco.com/marques/malabar/)). Carambar goes further into self-mockery. Its copy is riddle-then-pun ("Qui est jaune, drôle et complètement barré ?"), and even its statistics are jokes ("82 000 km de Carambar… Pour une fois, on ne blague pas !"). In 2017 it returned to TV with "Carambar, c'est de la barre !", explicitly borrowing YouTuber codes ([Carambar & Co – Carambar](https://carambarco.com/marques/carambar/)). "Blague Carambar" has entered French as a phrase for a corny joke ([Babylangues](https://job-in-france.babylangues.com/french-language/blagues-carambar/)). That is the highest compliment a brand voice can get.

Global players solve the dual-audience problem differently. They let adults be childish. Haribo's "Kids' Voices" platform (since 2014, close to 50 executions) dubs adults in boardrooms and libraries with children's voices ([LBB](https://lbbonline.com/news/haribos-successful-uk-kids-voices-campaign-gets-us-launch)). The agency and brand claim it regularly scored in the top 1% of tested ads, and System1 rated the 2024 "Fishing" spot 4.0 stars ([System1](https://system1group.com/?p=17385)). Oreo's "Stay Playful" frames its ritual as "no one is too old for fun" ([FoodNavigator-USA](https://www.foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2019/02/07/Oreo-s-new-Stay-Playful-campaign-taps-Wiz-Khalifa-and-his-son-to-show-no-one-is-too-old-for-fun)). Kinder positions itself on "special moments when parents can connect with their children" and backs that with Applaydu, an ad-free app with parental controls ([Ferrero North America](https://ferreronorthamerica.com/news/kinder-joy-toys-come-to-life-with-applaydu)). Inference: the inner-child and family-connection framing is partly a regulatory adaptation. It lets these brands advertise in adult and family media while staying clear of under-12/13 rules.

Clothing and toy brands confirm the split from the other side. Parents buy clothes, so clothing brands speak almost entirely to parents. They use founder stories, durability proof ("Hanna-Me-Down quality", [Hanna Andersson](https://hannaandersson.com/our-story)) and, lately, candid humour about the mess of parenting: Carter's CMO argued that "sometimes the mess is the thing that brings the brightest smile to our faces" ([Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/marketing/unwrap-the-chaos-carters-new-cmo-pushes-for-more-emotional-relatable-marketing/)). Toy brands write the split into their briefs. Playmobil's 2025 "The World Is Your Playground" films address children's creativity while reaching parents through "emotional relevance in everyday family life" ([Spielwarenmesse](https://www.spielwarenmesse.de/en/mag/toy-market-news/playmobil-brand-campaign-the-world-is-your-playground-with-new-commercials/)). Purpose platforms such as LEGO "Play Unstoppable" (a survey of 61,500 parents and children, free workshops and a parenting guide) and Hot Wheels "Challenge Accepted" (growth mindset, citing Mattel research that 77% of 4–8-year-olds believe they can learn anything with effort) make the child the on-screen hero and the parent the persuasion target ([LEGO newsroom](https://www.lego.com/aboutus/news/2024/february/lego-play-unstoppable); [Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20241016547476/en/Hot-Wheels-Ignites-New-%E2%80%9CChallenge-Accepted%E2%80%9D-Platform-Dedicated-to-Building-Resilience-and-Fostering-a-Growth-Mindset-in-Children)). The best-documented commercial result of this approach is Barbie's 2015 "Imagine the Possibilities": #1 YouTube ad that October with 12.4M views and double-digit Q4 sales growth. That figure is self-reported in a Cannes Lions entry ([Love the Work](https://lovethework.com/work-awards/campaigns/imagine-the-possibilities-41046)).

Inference: Ballon Boum Boum can borrow both registers at once. The French "tu"-and-pun voice belongs on screen with the characters. The Kinder/LEGO register of reassurance and benefit belongs in the parent-facing layer: channel description, captions, the parent post that accompanies each episode. Evidence is thin here: none of these sources compares the effectiveness of kid-addressed and parent-addressed tone head to head.

## Origin myths, rituals and sound outlive the mascots

Kids' candy brands tell their founding stories as short, self-mocking "happy accidents", not solemn heritage. Carambar was born "d'après la légende, d'une simple erreur de recette. Chez nous, on appelle ça une erreur de génie !" ([Carambar & Co – Carambar](https://carambarco.com/marques/carambar/)). Haribo tells "a humble home kitchen" story and an acronym ([Haribo history](https://www.haribo.com/en-au/about-us/history)). Chupa Chups put a sweet on a stick because sweets were messy for children ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chupa_Chups)). Your Episode 5 canon, in which the pink monster is rejected as a "défaut" and his difference becomes what makes every Boum Boum special, is structurally the same as Carambar's "erreur de génie". That is a strong validation, with one nuance from the evidence: these myths work because they are short and repeatable, and they get retold on packs, at anniversaries and on history pages. Inference: "Ce qui te rend différent, c'est ce qui te rend unique" is worth treating as a brand line, not a one-off episode moral. The toy world adds a caution: a heritage story must be true. Sophie la girafe's "made in France" aura took a hit when reports surfaced that production had moved to China ([Wikipedia citing Mediapart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_the_Giraffe)).

The strongest pattern in the candy evidence is **character + tactile ritual + sharing loop + playground competition**. When a brand removes the ritual, the audience pushes back. In 2012 Topps replaced Bazooka Joe's 59-year-old wrapper comics with codes for online games to stay "relevant to today's kids". Sales had already fallen from $17M (2007) to a projected $8.8M, and the comics later came back because they were what customers asked for most ([The New Republic](https://newrepublic.com/article/110868/bazooka-joe-comics-pulled-bubble-gum-after-58-years); [CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/1.6824358)). That is correlation, not proof of cause. Carambar ran the experiment on purpose. Its March 2013 "fin des blagues" hoax set off petitions and mobilised the factory town, and reportedly generated about 55,000 tweets and close to 1,000 media mentions with no paid media before the brand revealed the joke ([ozap](https://www.ozap.com/actu/les-blagues-carambar-c-est-fini/446200); [Guichet du savoir](https://www.guichetdusavoir.org/question/voir/134780)). Mascots, by contrast, get swapped. Malabar's Monsieur Malabar retired after 42 years in favour of Mabulle, a cat whose mission is "officiellement, t'apporter un maximum de fun. Officieusement, faire le pitre !", and Minions took over the packs for a year in 2018 ([Carambar & Co – Malabar](https://carambarco.com/marques/malabar/)). Changing how a mascot looks is risky because fans treat characters as people. M&M's 2022 redesign became a culture-war flashpoint and forced a staged "pause" ([Food Manufacturing](https://www.foodmanufacturing.com/consumer-trends/news/22670912/mars-pauses-using-mms-spokescandies)).

Sound is the most durable asset of all, and here the closest precedent is uncomfortably close. India's Boomer gum ran "Boom Boom Boomer" for about 20 years with a superhero mascot, Boomer Man, who "rescues kids from sticky situations". The brand later moved to teens, dropped the mascot and **kept the jingle** ([Social Samosa](https://www.socialsamosa.com/2020/09/brand-saga-boomer-advertising-journey)). Inference: a "BOUM" sound signature is the right instinct for a brand called Ballon Boum Boum. Have trademark counsel check for confusion with Boomer's "Boom Boom" jingle in any market where both are sold. The research does not establish where that overlap exists.

Bubble gum also has its own visual language. Bubble size and exaggeration appear in campaigns that have nothing to do with each other: a Big Babol bubble as a parachute in India, bubbles swamping a playground in Indonesia, a chameleon chasing coloured bubbles in Vietnam ([Campaign Asia](https://www.campaignasia.com/article/big-babol-crow-india/mzju1yn6x38aj8cob4chq4tjuz); [PostKiwi](https://www.postkiwi.com/?p=3275); [Campaign Asia](https://www.campaignasia.com/article/bubble-fun-campaign-marks-first-tv-export-from-batesasia-141-vietnam/202111)). Recent relaunches are TikTok-first and creator-led. Bubbaloo Colombia reported +24% brand recall, a platform-reported figure ([TikTok](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/inspiration/Bubbaloo-halloween-posicionamiento)). Character lore travels too: Squishmallows' name-and-bio hangtags turned toys into social currency, with "almost all organic" growth past 100M units ([Toy Book](https://toybook.com/?p=593506)). The evidence gap matters here. I found no verified tone or campaign source for Big Babol in France, the Middle East or the Maghreb, which are Ballon Boum Boum's closest competitive context, and no Effie-grade sales evidence for kids' candy storytelling in France or MENA.

## Deep kids' stories use one small feeling, a visible try and a repeatable hook

The craft evidence lines up with your standing rule. Bluey, Pixar shorts, Daniel Tiger and T'choupi all build an episode around one small, real emotional situation seen from the child's point of view, and resolve it with warmth and a coping move rather than a moral. Joe Brumm wanted parents to *enjoy* watching "rather than you just sort of tolerate it" ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2022/11/29/1139765338), via search summary). T'choupi's creator frames the books as a way for parents to "dédramatiser" everyday situations ([Vikidia](https://fr.vikidia.org/wiki/T%27choupi)). Pixar's story rules give the micro-structure. Rule #1: "You admire a character for trying more than for their successes." Rule #7: write the ending first. Rule #19: coincidences may get characters into trouble but must never get them out ([Aerogramme](https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/07/pixars-22-rules-of-storytelling/)). "Piper" shows how a wordless short carries one want and one fear ([/Film](https://www.slashfilm.com/543629/pixar-short-piper/)). Kenn Adams' Story Spine ("Every day… But one day… Because of that… Until finally… And ever since then…") is the skeleton under all of it ([NPR, 2026](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5750619)).

Two craft findings matter most for a candy series. First, a repeatable verbal or musical hook becomes the memory anchor. Daniel Tiger turns each lesson into a sung strategy ("take a deep breath and count to four") ([ALSC](https://www.alsc.ala.org/blog/?p=33027)), and Simon's "Caca boudin !" anchors a series sold into 200+ territories ([CNC](https://www.cnc.fr/series-tv/actualites/simon-le-petit-lapin-qui-seduit-a-linternational_1379862)). A secondhand-reported study found Daniel Tiger's empathy gains appeared **only when parents talked about the show with their child** ([okdiario summary](https://okdiario.com/metabolic/en/psychology/this-cartoon-teaches-kids-emotional-intelligence-and-social-confidence-according-to-a-study-18609/), a low-tier source). Inference: the parent layer is part of how the lesson works, not just a compliance hedge. Second, pacing is a child-development issue. Nine minutes of a fast-paced cartoon measurably impaired four-year-olds' executive function in a randomised study ([Lillard & Peterson, Pediatrics 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21911349/)). The AAP criticises CoComelon-style pacing and recommends call-and-response, repetition, slow pacing and songs caregivers can join ([AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/watching-cocomelon-and-guidance-on-early-childhood-development/)). Humour develops with age: slapstick and exaggeration land at 4–6, while wordplay and logical incongruity arrive later ([Frontiers 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2024.1369638/full)).

Inference for a 30–45 s vertical episode: a micro-spine of about six beats. Hook a face and a problem in 0–2 s, readable without sound. Show the "every day" ritual. "But one day" brings the feeling. Then a rule-of-three try and fail, a real choice, and a payoff that cuts back to the opening frame so it loops. Hold emotional shots 2–4 s rather than cutting every second or two. Oggy et les Cafards shows that pantomime with minimal dialogue travels across markets, India included ([CNC](https://www.cnc.fr/series-tv/actualites/oggy-et-les-cafards--retour-sur-une-incroyable-reussite-a-la-francaise_904379)). That matters for a French-language series that will play in Arabic-speaking GCC markets. Evidence is thin here too: I found no sourced retention or hook-timing data specific to kids' Shorts or TikTok, so "hook in 2 s" is industry practice, not proof.

## Four regimes converge on one rule: the gum can appear, but it cannot be the hero

**Europe and France.** The EU Pledge counts social media, company-owned websites, YouTube and contracted creators as covered media. Members may not advertise non-qualifying products where 30% or more of the audience is under 13. Where audience data are missing, they must judge by the "overall impression" and exclude content primarily appealing to under-13s, naming "cartoons… or YouTube channels" ([EU Pledge](https://eu-pledge.eu/our-commitment/)). The mascot loophole is narrow. "Company-owned, brand equity characters are not covered" by the character restriction ([EU Pledge](https://eu-pledge.eu/our-commitment/)), but the series as a whole remains a marketing communication. The ICC framework applies whatever the audience: no encouraging excess, appropriate portions, honest depiction, snacks never as meal substitutes ([ICC Framework 2019](https://iccwbo.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2019/08/icc-framework-for-responsible-food-and-beverage-marketing-communications-2019.pdf)). France adds the most script-specific code anywhere: **ARPP "Comportements alimentaires" v4, applicable from 15 June 2026**. It covers characters "imaginaires ou pas". It bans eating "sans retenue", all-day snacking and eating in front of a screen at home, and it requires breakfast and goûter to be shown as balanced. It bans implying exceptional school, art or sport performance from a food, while allowing humour and imaginary worlds "clearly in the register of fantasy". It forbids undermining the authority of adults around the child, and it bans food-waste scenes and body-shape stigma ([ARPP v4](https://www.arpp.org/nous-consulter/regles/regles-de-deontologie/recommandation-comportements-alimentaires-v4/)). The ARPP Enfant rules ban exploiting credulity, urgency, price minimising ("seulement") and suggestions that owning the product gives social advantage ([ARPP Reco Enfant](https://www.arpp.org/IMG/pdf/Reco_Enfant.pdf)). French law forbids directly inciting minors to buy or to persuade their parents to buy ([Assemblée nationale](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/15/questions/QANR5L15QE18540.pdf)). Food advertising must carry the health message with a mangerbouger.fr reference for its full duration, or pay a 1.5% levy ([Eurojuris](https://www.eurojuris.fr/consommation/articles/mentions-sanitaires-dans-publicite-alimentaire-6291.htm)). The Arcom 2025–2029 food charter now includes Google, Meta, TikTok and Snapchat ([CB News](https://www.cbnews.fr/node/89516)). Health NGOs complain that it has stalled ([France Assos Santé](https://france-assos-sante.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/CP-Bilan-Charte-Arcom-23-fev.-2026-mis-en-forme.pdf)), which itself signals political pressure for tougher rules.

**United Kingdom.** Since 5 January 2026, paid online ads for identifiable less-healthy products, confectionery included, have been banned outright. Owned media such as organic posts on the brand's own channel are out of scope, **but the brand "cannot pay to boost or promote that content"**, and paying creators or gifting counts as paid ([IAB UK](https://www.iabuk.com/news-article/less-healthy-food-and-drink-ad-ban-what-it-how-it-works)). Brand-only ads are exempt if they depict no specific product. "Depict" includes jingles, audio cues and "clues such as colours or ingredients", while brand logos, colours and straplines are allowed ([IAB UK](https://www.iabuk.com/news-article/less-healthy-food-and-drink-ad-ban-what-it-how-it-works)). The CAP Code still forbids targeting HFSS ads at under-16s. Licensed characters popular with children are banned in HFSS ads aimed at primary-age children, while brand-owned equity characters are allowed ([ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/children-food.html)). The 2024 Garfield/Cheestrings ruling shows how strictly the licensed-character rule is enforced ([ASA ruling](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/kerry-foods-ltd-a24-1244924-kerry-foods-ltd.html)). Inference with real uncertainty: a pink, strawberry-flavoured monster "born in the candy factory" may itself read as a product "clue" in paid UK media. Pastèque-flavour episodes certainly would. No ruling in the notes tests a mascot that *is* the flavour.

**GCC and Maghreb.** Rules here are fragmented but tightening. Saudi Arabia's SFDA notified the WTO in 2024 of a draft regulation on food marketing directed at children, covering **under-18s**. Its final adoption is unconfirmed, and earlier voluntary guidelines used under-12 ([Food Compliance International](https://foodcomplianceinternational.com/industry-insight/news/4831-sfda-proposes-regulation-on-marketing-requirements-for-foods-intended-for-children); [USDA FAIRS](https://agriexchange.apeda.gov.in/ImportRegulations/FAIRS%20Annual%20Country%20Report%20AnnualRiyadhSaudi%20ArabiaSA20230004.pdf)). SFDA also pre-approves food ads ([Karger](https://karger.com/sjh/article/3/1-4/147/856206/Food-Advertised-to-Children-A-Content-Analysis)). In the UAE, GCC Food & Beverage Alliance members pledge not to target children aged 12 and under with high-sugar products ([The National](https://thenationalnews.com/opinion/the-case-to-limit-junk-food-adverts-1.17331)). Abu Dhabi announced a fast-food ad ban in February 2026 within a broader HFSS framework ([Communicate Online](https://communicateonline.me/news/fast-food-advertising-banned-across-abu-dhabi-under-major-health-drive/)). Morocco's HACA has prohibited a snack ad for exploiting children's credulity ([HACA](https://haca.ma/fr/printpdf/3510)). I found no food-specific rules for Tunisia or Algeria, so the evidence is thin for the Maghreb.

**Platforms.** YouTube's kids-and-family quality principles apply to Shorts and list "heavy promotion of products or brands" as low quality. Such content is demoted and can lose monetisation ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/10774223?hl=en), via search summary). Made-for-kids videos lose comments and personalised ads. Paid promotions in them need a disclosure children can understand ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/10588440)), and flagged paid placements are excluded from the YouTube Kids app ([Markey/YouTube letter](https://www.markey.senate.gov/download/youtube-kids-ads?download=1)). TikTok says HFSS ads must not target users aged 16 and under or carry a specific call to purchase. In several markets, branded content for children's products is restricted to 18+ audiences and must not "look overly childish" ([Geelong Indy](https://geelongindy.com.au/?p=76477); [TikTok](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/branded-content-policy-country-specific-requirements?lang=TH)). WHO's 2023 guideline points every regime toward mandatory protection of all under-18s and names cartoons as a persuasive technique ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news/item/03-07-2023-who-recommends-stronger-policies-to-protect-children-from-the-harmful-impact-of-food-marketing); [Down To Earth](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/news/food/who-releases-policy-recommendations-to-protect-children-from-harmful-effects-of-food-marketing-90393)).

Inference, and the central strategic point: these regimes differ in thresholds, but they converge on the same creative answer. The less an episode depends on showing, naming or consuming the gum, the more places it can legally run, be boosted and be monetised. Episodes 6 (letting a friend go home) and 8 (kindness brings colour back) are already that kind of episode. A strategic unknown changes the calculus: the notes do not say whether Ballon Boum Boum's owner is an EU Pledge, IFBA or GCC F&B Alliance signatory, or which markets the series will be targeted at. Those facts determine how much of the above is binding commitment and how much is best practice.

## What this means for Les Aventures Boum Boum

Your existing slate is well aligned with the depth evidence and needs only targeted compliance care. The table below assesses each episode from its title and premise. I have not seen the scripts, so this is inference.

| Episode | Fit with research | Risk to check in the script |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Le plus gros BOUM | Native bubble-gum trope (bubble size, playground contest) | The bubble's power must stay fantastical and must not be shown as real performance from chewing (ARPP v4 2/1); one piece, not a mouthful |
| 2 Boum-Boo (Halloween) | Seasonal ritual, mild fear theme | No candy hoarding or piles, no "can't stop eating"; adults respected |
| 3 Le Duel de Bulles | Competition ritual (Malabar "concours") | Winning must come from effort or ingenuity, not from owning the gum (ARPP Enfant: no social superiority) |
| 4 Joyeux anniversaire Mélo | Celebration is a legitimate treat occasion | Show one shared portion, not candy as the meal |
| 5 La Naissance de Boum Boum | Mirrors Carambar's "erreur de génie"; a strong brand myth | Keep it short and retellable; factory scenes must not glamorise quantities |
| 6 L'Étoile tombée | Product-free emotional story (Bluey/Piper model) | Lowest risk; best candidate for paid promotion |
| 7 Le Jardin de Mélo | Patience (CASEL self-management) | Origin of the Pastèque *flavour* is product depiction: treat as a product episode (UK "clues", EU Pledge), and do not boost it |
| 8 Le Pays sans couleurs | Kindness, prosocial; low product dependence | Low risk; make sure colour returns through kindness, not through gum |

The end card needs one deliberate decision. "Fais BOUM !" is an imperative addressed to children. If it sits next to a pack shot, it reads as a direct exhortation to consume, which French law and ARPP Enfant forbid when aimed at minors. If it means a **gesture and a sound** (a two-fist "BOUM" with a bubble pop sound, which a child can do with no gum at all), it becomes a ritual like the Carambar joke: owned, shareable and product-independent. Inference: define "Fais BOUM !" as that gesture in the bible, keep the pack off the end card, and pair it with a disclosure such as "Une série Ballon Boum Boum".

### 1. Tone-of-voice guide for Ballon Boum Boum

Inference throughout, grounded in the Malabar/Carambar register, the Kinder/LEGO/Tonies parent register, and the ARPP/ICC rules cited above.

**To kids (on screen, in character).** Use the playground "tu" with puns, sound effects and cheeky self-mockery: brave, kind, a little ridiculous. The monster is warm and enthusiastic. Mélo is cool and dry, with a half-smile, and his jokes land for older kids and parents. Humour is mostly physical (bubbles, pops, faces) with one visual or verbal pun per episode for 7–10-year-olds. Feelings are named simply. The gum is a friendship object, never a power-up, a prize or a status symbol.

**To parents (descriptions, captions, parent posts, website).** Speak as a calm, honest, slightly self-deprecating adult who admits the obvious ("oui, on fait du chewing-gum"). Say what each episode is about emotionally and give one question to ask the child. Be open about the series being brand content. Never moralise, and never pretend gum is healthy.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| "Toi aussi, t'as déjà eu peur d'un gros BOUM ?" | "Demande à tes parents de t'en acheter !" |
| "Inspire… souffle… BOUM !" (a calming ritual) | "Avec Boum Boum, tu gagnes à tous les coups !" |
| "Une bulle, ça se partage." | "Mange-en autant que tu veux !" / "Sans modération !" |
| "Ce qui te rend différent, c'est ce qui te rend unique." | "Tout le monde en a, et toi ?" |
| "Fais BOUM !" as a gesture, with no pack in frame | "Seulement 1 € !" / "Vite, avant qu'il n'y en ait plus !" |
| Parent caption: "Cette semaine : attendre, c'est dur. Demandez à votre enfant ce qu'il a eu du mal à attendre aujourd'hui." | Characters outwitting or ignoring a grown-up to get gum |
| Parent caption: "Une série Ballon Boum Boum. Le chewing-gum, c'est un plaisir de temps en temps : on ne le mange pas devant un écran." | Gum eaten while watching a tablet or TV at home, or shown as goûter or breakfast |
| Mélo, deadpan: "Moi ? Peur ? … Un tout petit peu." | Jokes about body size, or wasting or throwing gum |

### 2. Brand story and storytelling framework

**Universe (evidence-backed pattern, applied).** The candy factory is the world, with fixed locations: the factory floor, Mélo's garden, the rooftop at night. Each new flavour arrives as a *character with a bio*, following the Squishmallows/Beanie Babies lore model ([Toy Book](https://toybook.com/squishmallows-specialty-toys-feature/)), but with no rarity or collect-them-all pressure (CARU/ARPP). The founding myth is Episode 5, retold in one line wherever the brand speaks: "Il était un défaut. Il est devenu le cœur de chaque Boum Boum." That is the "erreur de génie" pattern.

**Episode engine.** Use the micro-spine from the craft section: one feeling, a visible try and fail (Pixar #1), a real choice, a payoff that loops. The rule from Pixar #19 becomes a brand rule: **the gum never rescues the hero**. Courage, patience, kindness or a friend does.

**Rituals and catchphrases.** Use three recurring devices and keep them stable for years. That consistency is what made the Haribo voice, the Carambar joke and the Boomer jingle valuable. The first is "Fais BOUM !", the end-card gesture plus a signature pop sound: the audio logo. The second is "Inspire… souffle… BOUM !", a Daniel Tiger-style strategy chant for big feelings, sung at the turning point when an episode calls for it. The third is "Ce qui te rend différent, c'est ce qui te rend unique", the brand belief, used sparingly at emotional peaks.

**Recurring formats.** Run three. The core story episodes (30–45 s, one idea each). A "Qui est-ce ?" character-card micro-format (10–15 s, a name, a trait and a birthday for each factory friend). A seasonal special slot (Halloween already exists). The Carambar co-creation precedent (jokes sourced from schoolchildren, [Lingua Franca](https://www.mylinguafranca.com/le-chouchou-news/2021/9/11/blagues-carambar)) suggests a later parent-mediated "Ton BOUM" call for drawings. Run it only through parent-facing channels, and never in exchange for purchases.

### 4. Five new episode ideas, and which to make first

**A. "Le Souffle de Boum Boum" (recommended first).** Logline: when his tower of bubble-blocks collapses, the monster's heart glows red-hot and he wants to BOUM everything. Mélo shows him that a bubble only grows if you blow *slowly*, and the same slow breath shrinks the angry glow. Lesson: managing anger by breathing (CASEL self-management). Ritual: it introduces "Inspire… souffle… BOUM !" as a strategy chant that children can do with an imaginary bubble, no gum needed. Inspired by: Daniel Tiger's strategy songs and the parent-talk finding ([ALSC](https://www.alsc.ala.org/blog/?p=33027)), the AAP's call for slow, joinable rituals ([AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/watching-cocomelon-and-guidance-on-early-childhood-development/)), and the glowing-heart canon. Why first: it creates a product-free ritual that parents will value, it can be boosted in every market (UK brand exemption, YouTube quality rules), and it gives "BOUM" a meaning beyond consumption, which makes the end card defensible.

**B. "Le jour où le BOUM a disparu".** Logline: one morning the factory's BOUM sound is gone. Bubbles pop silently and nobody laughs. Mélo, who used to fear the BOUM, is the one who goes looking for it. Lesson: sometimes we only realise how much we love something when it's gone, and courage can be done for others. Ritual: the restored "Fais BOUM !" sound becomes the climax. Inspired by: Carambar's 2013 "fin des blagues" hoax and Bazooka's comic removal ([ozap](https://www.ozap.com/actu/les-blagues-carambar-c-est-fini/446200); [The New Republic](https://newrepublic.com/article/110868/bazooka-joe-comics-pulled-bubble-gum-after-58-years)). It should air after Mélo's fear arc resolves, as its payoff.

**C. "Qui est-ce ?" character-card micro-series.** Logline: each 15-second card introduces a factory friend (name, one quirk, birthday) through a tiny moment with the monster. Lesson: curiosity about people who are different from you, extending Ep. 5's belief. Ritual: a recurring card format and catchphrase. Inspired by: Squishmallows bios and Beanie Babies birthdays as kid-led social currency ([Toy Book](https://toybook.com/squishmallows-specialty-toys-feature/)). Guardrail: characters, not SKUs, and no rarity or collecting pressure. Flavour-linked characters count as product depiction in the UK.

**D. "Le Grand Fatigué".** Logline: the old confectioner who runs the factory is too tired to finish the night shift. The monster and Mélo want to surprise him, try to do everything themselves and make a mess. They learn that helping means asking what he needs. Lesson: grown-ups have feelings too, and helping is listening. Ritual: a quiet "Fais BOUM !" whispered so as not to wake him, a gag that also lands for parents. Inspired by: Bluey's dual-audience parenting truths "in a very G-rated way" ([Laughing Place](https://www.laughingplace.com/w/news/2023/06/09/bluey-creator-joe-brumm-opens-up-about-creation-of-the-series-disneys-initial-desire-to-change-characters-accents/)), Carter's candid "mess" humour ([Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/marketing/unwrap-the-chaos-carters-new-cmo-pushes-for-more-emotional-relatable-marketing/)), and ARPP v4 2/2 on respecting adults' authority.

**E. "La Nuit du Partage" (seasonal, for GCC/Maghreb).** Logline: on a long, quiet night the monster has one bubble left and many friends waiting. He discovers that a bubble shared, blown together, gets bigger than any he could blow alone. Lesson: generosity and togetherness. Ritual: a two-friend "BOUM" where the bubble is a shared act and only one portion appears. Inspired by: Babyshop's Ramadan films, told "through the eyes and the words of children" and reported as earning a 10:1 profit ROI with a WARC MENA prize ([WARC](https://www.warc.com/content/article/Babyshop_World_Without_Walls/126225); [Campaign ME](https://campaignme.com/fp7-mccann-creates-a-world-we-deserve-for-babyshop/)). Guardrail: keep it culturally universal rather than religious-specific unless local partners guide it, and note that Morocco's regulator watches Ramadan advertising.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what Ballon Boum Boum actually owns. Its asset is not the gum on screen. It is a sound, a gesture and a belief ("ce qui te rend différent…") that children can carry into the playground with no product in hand, the way generations carried the Carambar joke. The regulatory trend, from ARPP v4 to the UK ban to Saudi Arabia's under-18 draft and WHO's push on cartoons, makes that ritual-first, product-light approach the only one that scales across your markets. The craft evidence says it is also the version children and parents will love. Your rule of "deep, one idea, real choice" is therefore a commercial strategy as much as an artistic one.

Three questions should be answered before production scales, because the evidence cannot answer them: whether the brand owner is bound by the EU Pledge, IFBA or the GCC pledge; whether regulators would treat a flavour-coloured, factory-born mascot as a depiction of the product itself; and which markets each episode will actually be targeted and boosted in. Settle those, lock "Fais BOUM !" as a gesture rather than an instruction to eat, and produce "Le Souffle de Boum Boum" first. It is the episode that turns the brand's name into something a child does when they feel big feelings, and it is cleared to travel everywhere.
